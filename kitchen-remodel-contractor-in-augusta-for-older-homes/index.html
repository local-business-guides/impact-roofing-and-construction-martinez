What should a kitchen remodel contractor cover?

A kitchen remodel can include planning the layout, removing old cabinets and finishes, installing new cabinetry and counters, updating lighting and flooring, and coordinating plumbing or electrical work. The scope depends on what you want changed and what the existing room can support.

A contractor should explain which tasks are included, which trades will handle them, and what is excluded. Ask for those details in a written proposal, along with how changes to the plan will be approved and documented.

What should a local professional check in an older home?

Before settling on a design, a contractor should look at the kitchen's current layout, visible plumbing and electrical conditions, floors and walls, ventilation, and how cabinets and appliances fit. Older homes can have uneven surfaces or hidden conditions that only become clear once work begins, so the initial plan should explain how unexpected issues will be handled.

It also helps to discuss which parts of the house will be affected while the kitchen is out of use. Ask how the crew plans to protect nearby rooms, manage dust and debris, and keep a clear path through the home.

What drives the cost of an older-home kitchen remodel?

The price depends on the size of the room, the amount of demolition, the cabinets and counters selected, appliance choices, flooring, lighting, and whether the layout changes. Moving a sink, range, or major appliances can add work because plumbing, electrical, or ventilation may need changes too.

The condition of what is behind the finished surfaces matters as well. Repairs to damaged subflooring, walls, plumbing, or wiring can affect the final scope, as can permit requirements and the cost of disposing of old materials. Ask the contractor to separate known work from allowances or items that cannot be confirmed until demolition.

No responsible estimate can account perfectly for every concealed condition before work opens up the room. Before hiring, make sure the proposal says how the contractor will price and approve extra work, and that you will have a chance to review it before the work proceeds.

How can you keep the timeline realistic?

Timing is shaped by the scope, product availability, required inspections, trade schedules, and what the crew finds during demolition. Custom or backordered materials can also affect when installation can happen, so ask which selections need to be finalized early.

Request a written schedule that shows the expected work sequence, major decision dates, and how delays will be communicated. For an older home, ask how the plan may change if repairs are needed, and what parts of the kitchen will be unavailable during each stage.

What mistakes should you avoid before work starts?

  • Choosing based only on the lowest total without comparing what each proposal includes.
  • Assuming the existing plumbing, wiring, or floors can be reused without an inspection.
  • Ordering cabinets or appliances before confirming measurements, clearances, and the final layout.
  • Starting demolition before confirming who is responsible for permits, disposal, and trade coordination.
  • Agreeing to extra work verbally without a written description and approval of the added cost.

A clear proposal and a steady record of decisions can prevent many avoidable disagreements. Keep copies of selections, approvals, schedule updates, and any changes to the original scope.

What local rules should you check, and when should you call a pro?

Permit requirements depend on the work being done and the applicable local rules. Ask the contractor which permits or inspections may apply to your project, who will obtain them, and whether the work requires coordination with separate licensed trades. Confirm requirements with the appropriate local building department rather than assuming a cosmetic-looking change is automatically exempt.

Bring in a qualified professional before work begins if you plan to move plumbing or electrical connections, change walls, or address damage or unsafe conditions. If demolition reveals a problem outside the agreed scope, pause that part of the work and get the condition, proposed fix, cost, and schedule effect in writing.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Have you worked on kitchens in older homes with a similar scope?
  • What work is included in this proposal, and what is specifically excluded?
  • Who will coordinate the plumbing, electrical, and other trade work?
  • Who is responsible for permits and arranging required inspections?
  • How will you protect the rest of the home and manage dust and debris?
  • How do you document and price unexpected repairs or changes?
  • What is the expected schedule, and how will you tell me about delays?
  • Who will be my main contact while the project is underway?

Listen for clear, consistent answers and make sure important commitments appear in the written agreement. If a provider is vague about exclusions, extra work, or who is responsible for permits, ask for clarification before you sign.
