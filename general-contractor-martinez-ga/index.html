What should a general contractor handle?

A general contractor typically helps organize a project from the agreed scope through completion. Depending on the job, that may include coordinating trades, scheduling work, ordering materials, communicating about decisions, and keeping the work aligned with the plans and contract.

The written agreement should spell out what the contractor is taking responsibility for and what remains yours to arrange. Look for the work areas, materials or finish selections, cleanup, schedule assumptions, permit responsibilities, payment terms, and a process for approving changes. If a task is not described, ask whether it is included before work begins.

A general contractor is not automatically responsible for every issue found on site. Hidden damage, outdated systems, or changes to the original plan can affect the scope. The contract should explain how those conditions will be documented and priced before additional work proceeds.

What does a local professional check before giving a plan?

A useful first review starts with your goals and the existing conditions, not just a quick estimate. The contractor needs to understand what you want to change, how the space is used, what must stay in place, and whether there are known problems behind walls, under flooring, or around the roof and exterior.

  • The exact rooms, surfaces, or exterior areas included in the work.
  • Plans, measurements, photos, and any prior inspection or repair information you can share.
  • Material and fixture choices that affect ordering, fit, or installation.
  • Access, delivery, parking, and how the household can use the property while work is underway.
  • Whether permits, inspections, association approval, or other sign-offs may apply.

For Martinez projects, ask the contractor to identify which local office or association should confirm the rules for your specific address and type of work. A general answer is not a substitute for checking the requirements that apply to the property.

What changes the cost of a general contractor project?

The price depends on the work being done, the materials selected, and the conditions the crew finds. A bathroom remodel with changes to plumbing or layout is a different scope from replacing finishes in the same footprint. For a larger project, design decisions, structural work, and the number of trades involved can add planning and coordination.

Ask whether the proposal separates labor, materials, allowances, and work that is not included. Allowances can change when you choose the actual fixture or finish, and concealed damage may require a revised scope. No responsible estimate can account for every hidden condition before the affected area is opened.

To compare bids, give each provider the same plans, selections, and expectations. Ask what assumptions the estimate makes, how changes are approved, and whether disposal, protection of nearby areas, and final cleanup are included. Avoid comparing totals alone when the described work differs.

What can affect the schedule?

Timing can depend on the size and sequence of the work, trade availability, material lead times, inspections, and decisions that still need to be made. Existing conditions may also change the plan once demolition or repair work reveals what is behind the visible surface.

Ask for a projected start and completion window, along with the milestones that matter for your job. Find out when selections must be finalized, how delays will be communicated, and what happens if work cannot continue as planned. A clear schedule should identify assumptions rather than promise a date without explaining what it depends on.

Which local rules should you check?

Permit and inspection requirements depend on the kind of work and the property. Before signing, ask who will check with the appropriate building office, who will apply for any required permits, and who will arrange inspections. Confirm the answer for your address instead of assuming every repair or remodel follows the same process.

If your home is subject to an owners association or other neighborhood guidelines, ask whether its approval is needed for exterior changes, additions, or visible materials. Clarify who gathers the documents and whether work should wait until approval is in hand.

What mistakes should you avoid before hiring?

  • Choosing from the total alone without checking whether each proposal covers the same work.
  • Relying on a verbal promise instead of putting scope, exclusions, schedule, and payment terms in writing.
  • Assuming permits, inspections, disposal, or protection of the home are included without asking.
  • Approving extra work before you understand its cost and effect on timing.
  • Paying without a clear written agreement that explains payment milestones and how changes are handled.
  • Skipping references, relevant project examples, or verification of insurance and any credentials required for the work.

If a proposal is vague, ask for clarification before you commit. A contractor who can explain what is known, what is uncertain, and how changes will be handled gives you a better basis for making a decision.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What work is included, and what is specifically excluded?
  • Who will manage the project and be my main contact?
  • Which trades will be involved, and who coordinates their work?
  • Who checks permit requirements and handles applications or inspections if they apply?
  • What materials, finishes, or allowances are included in this proposal?
  • How do I approve a change, and how will it affect the cost and schedule?
  • What is the expected schedule, and which decisions or deliveries could change it?
  • Can you provide relevant project examples and confirm the insurance or credentials needed for this work?

Questions people ask about general contractor Martinez GA

What does it mean to be a contractor for a company?

It usually means the contractor performs work for a business under an agreement, rather than being that business's employee. For a homeowner, the important point is to clarify which company or individual is responsible for the work, communication, and any subcontractors.

What do contractors charge per hour?

There is no single hourly rate that applies to every trade or project, and many contractors price a defined job rather than bill only by the hour. Ask the provider how labor, materials, allowances, and extra work are billed.

Is a contractor the same as a builder?

The terms can overlap, but they do not always describe the same role. A builder often focuses on construction, while a general contractor may coordinate several parts of a project; check the provider's written scope rather than relying on the title.

What terms describe this type of work?

Depending on the job, people may say builder, construction professional, or service provider. Those labels are not interchangeable in every situation, so ask what work the person or company is actually agreeing to perform.
